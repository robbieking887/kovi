Why overloading is a problem

  • Longer stopping distances and weaker steering.
  • Tyres, brakes and suspension work harder and fail sooner.
  • It is an offence, and an overloaded vehicle can be given a prohibition and lead to fines for the driver and the operator.
  • Insurance may be affected after an incident.

Know your limits

  • Gross vehicle weight (the maximum the vehicle can weigh fully loaded) is on the plate.
  • Payload is what the vehicle can carry once you take off its own weight, the driver and any fuel and equipment.
  • Axle weights matter as well as the total. A load in the wrong place can overload one axle.

Our weights guide explains these terms.

How to prevent it

  • Give drivers the payload for each vehicle.
  • Weigh loads where there is doubt. Use weighbridges and onboard weighing where fitted.
  • Plan the load so weight is spread evenly and low.
  • Make it clear that drivers can refuse to drive an overloaded vehicle.

On the daily check

Look for sagging suspension, tyres bulging at the sidewall and a load that looks too high. Report them. See tyre checks.

General information, not legal advice or a substitute for official guidance. Check HSE, DVSA and GOV.UK for current requirements.

Questions

Who is responsible for an overloaded vehicle?

Both the driver and the operator can be held responsible. The details depend on the circumstances. See GOV.UK for the current rules.
